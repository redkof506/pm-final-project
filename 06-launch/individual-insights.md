## Friction points

_The biggest technical or strategic challenge you faced while developing your product concept._

The hardest part of building out the Spotlight initiative wasn't any single artifact — it was keeping the artifacts *honest with each other*. Once I had personas, journey maps, a MoSCoW-prioritized backlog, and an effort/value scoring model all in motion at the same time, it became easy for each one to quietly drift into its own logic. A feature could look like an obvious "Must Have" on the MoSCoW board while scoring only moderately on the effort/value matrix, and neither view on its own told me whether that was a real conflict or just two different lenses on the same trade-off. I had to keep going back to the persona and journey-map layer as the tie-breaker, forcing myself to ask "which specific friction, for which specific persona, does this actually resolve?" rather than letting the prioritization frameworks argue with each other in the abstract. Translating that resolved, defensible prioritization into an interactive HTML roadmap deliverable added a second layer of friction: the format itself demanded a level of precision (clear scope boundaries, unambiguous sequencing) that a slide or spreadsheet would have let me get away with skipping.

## Key learnings

_A few surprising discoveries or insights you gained from the course overall._

- **Personas earn their keep only when they're load-bearing.** It was easy to treat persona discovery as an upfront exercise to check off before "the real work" of prioritization started. The moment I made personas the actual arbiter of prioritization disputes — instead of a document I referred back to occasionally — the whole process got faster and the outputs got more defensible.
- **Journey maps expose friction that scoring models miss.** MoSCoW and effort/value scoring are good at ranking, but they don't naturally surface *where in the experience* a problem lives. Walking the journey map step by step surfaced a couple of pain points that never would have bubbled up from prioritization criteria alone, simply because no one had framed them as a discrete "step."
- **A prioritization framework is a conversation-starter, not a verdict.** I came in expecting MoSCoW and effort/value scoring to produce a clean, final answer. What they actually did was give me a structured way to have a disagreement — with stakeholders, and honestly with myself — about trade-offs that are ultimately judgment calls.
- **Format constraints are a design tool, not just an output.** Building the roadmap as an interactive HTML deliverable rather than a static slide forced a level of rigor (explicit sequencing, no hand-waved scope) that improved the underlying thinking, not just the presentation.

## Aha! moment

_Your main "aha" moment during the project process._

The real "aha" landed when I laid the journey map and the MoSCoW backlog side by side and realized the feature I had scored as highest-value on paper wasn't actually resolving the moment of highest friction in the persona's real journey — it was resolving a step that *looked* important because it was easy to describe, not because it was where the user actually got stuck. That was the moment prioritization stopped being an exercise in scoring rows on a spreadsheet and became an exercise in reading a story carefully enough to know which sentence in it actually needed rewriting. It reframed how I think about my own move toward more technical, data-facing PM work too: the frameworks and the pipelines are only as good as the discipline to keep checking them against the actual experience they're meant to serve.
